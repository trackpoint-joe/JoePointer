Rethinking Career Development at Scale Through AI

The Full Story
See how one 27-year associate's transformation shaped a program that reached the entire organization

The Story

An associate had spent 27 years processing distributions. When TIAA announced office closures affecting more than 1,000 associates, she was terrified. She couldn't see how to talk about her experience in a way another employer would value.

In one of my CareerSpark sessions, we used AI prompts to break down what it takes to be good at her job. The output wasn't one task. It was dozens of skills: problem solving, client relationship management, attention to detail, working under pressure, handling complexity, meeting deadlines, advocating for customers.

Watching that list appear, I could see the weight lift from her shoulders. She realized she had far more to offer than a job title suggested. That transformation is what CareerSpark enables at scale.

The Challenge

TIAA was in the middle of a major transformation, adopting new technology and rethinking how it works. Transformation like that only holds when the people inside it grow with it. How do we make sure associates have the skills, the mindset and the confidence to thrive in a rapidly changing environment? How do we make transformation personal, not just organizational?

On the ground, associates faced two major office closures affecting more than 1,000 people, limited visibility into their transferable skills and development plans that felt like guesswork. AI tools were being rolled out, but the training was highly technical and most associates did not see AI as "for people like them."

The Insight

Instead of teaching AI through abstract business cases, I taught AI through the one subject every associate is already an expert in: themselves.

Use AI as a guided mirror to help people see the skills they already have, explore where those skills could take them next and build language and artifacts (resumes, profiles, plans) that tell their story.

The Solution

I conceived and designed CareerSpark as an AI-enabled career development journey built around 9 integrated modules that carry someone from understanding their own value through to landing their next role.

The Framework Behind It: The 4 Pillars
The career model CareerSpark operationalizes and the personal wake-up that started it

The work started with my own resume. I sat with a career coach, told her about my fifteen years, and she said my resume did not tell the story I had just told her. She was right. I had the experience, but on paper it read as a list of tasks with no thread and no why. That gap is what sent me looking for a better way to help people connect their experience to where they want to go.

Out of that came the model I teach. A real development plan stands on four pillars:

  • Skills: what you can do
  • Visibility: who knows what you do
  • Network: who you learn from and grow with
  • Story: how you connect it all

Most people only work the first pillar. They take a course and call it a plan. The other three are where careers actually move.

CareerSpark puts all four into practice across its nine modules. You start with your values, not a job title, because a plan that does not start with what matters to you never feels like yours.

The Innovation Approach: Start With the Problem
Why CareerSpark started with the problem and how it solved two problems at once

A common failure in AI efforts is starting with the technology and hunting for a use case. I started the other way around. I asked what problem needed solving, designed the solution and used AI as the tool to build and scale it.

CareerSpark solved two problems at once. It was a career tool, and it was also a low-stakes way for associates to learn AI. Working through their own career questions, associates practiced how to prompt AI, judge what it gave back and iterate until the output reflected their real experience. The career problem was the on-ramp to using AI, and that is what drove adoption here.

The shift is from asking what AI can do to asking what problem needs solving. Using AI well starts with understanding the problem well enough to design the solution, then using AI to build it. The approach is repeatable:

  1. Identify a real business problem
  2. Quantify the impact
  3. Understand why current solutions fall short
  4. Design the solution before thinking about AI
  5. Evaluate AI as a tool
  6. Start small and iterate
  7. Scale and share
Learning Design and Ecosystem
How the 9-module journey was designed for people to actually use and the tools around it

CareerSpark was built as a product, not a course. Its nine modules carry someone from understanding their own value through to landing their next role, and each one is designed for a person in the middle of a busy day. The learning happens by doing. Associates work through their own real career questions with guided AI prompts, see worked examples and iterate until the output reflects their actual experience. That is also how the AI skill sinks in, through practice on something they already care about.

The ecosystem includes: Resources on ATS and keywords, tracking tools for job searches and a Manager Guide built with Amazon Q Developer in 1 week compared to months for a traditional team.

The Challenge & Approach
See how I treated modernization as change management wrapped in a facilities problem

The Challenge

When TIAA called more than 3,000 associates back to office in January 2022, the initiative launched multi-million dollar modernization projects across six locations. But modernization brought hard choices: associates would lose assigned seats, embrace hoteling models and see their personal workspace "homes away from home" redesigned without their input. Eighteen months of remote work had severed the connection to physical spaces.

How do you lead people through multiple unwanted changes simultaneously (modernization, seating model shifts, facility redesigns) when trust is already fragile? Get it right, and you modernize the company while keeping culture intact. Get it wrong, and you trigger a retention crisis.

The Approach

I treated it as a change management problem wrapped in a facilities problem. I built a structure where tactical work gets delegated (to space liaisons), strategic work stays centralized (relationship management with GCS, CRE, Facilities, HR, leadership), and one person stays constant through all the change. That was me.

Key Strategic Moves

  1. Built a liaison network: 8 space liaisons across locations managing day-to-day space planning; I removed obstacles and managed strategy
  2. Showed up personally: Attended every Client Services town hall; answered hard questions; was honest about changes I didn't like either
  3. Designed for adoption: Created neighborhoods in Zoom Workplaces for hoteling navigation; hosted campus tours for call center associates moving to Charlotte; made the case for digital displays and showed how to use them to engage associates in hoteling environments
  4. Maintained relationships: Regular monthly meetings with business area leaders showing how their requirements were reflected in designs; educated new GCS leaders on lessons from prior projects
  5. Stayed constant: more than 12 years as Workspace Leader; my continuity became a form of leadership capital through all the changes

That approach became a repeatable model: the same approach got displays installed at every call center location, and they were built in as a standard requirement for the new Frisco Corporate Center.

Denver Consolidation & Modernization (2023-2024): 700 associates transitioning from assigned to hoteling seating; achieved 44% more efficient space utilization. Most resistive change; I volunteered to attend townhalls and answer every concern. Result: minimal unexpected turnover.

Frisco Corporate Center Opening (2024): Led Client Services change management for more than 500 CS associates moving into new 15-story building; largest modernization undertaking. Negotiated special call center configuration for team cohesion. Recognized as "Change Champion."

Future Me, Answered

AI Career Discovery for the Next Generation

900+ Career Paths Surfaced
5 Stage Journey
4 Paths, Weighted Equally

The deeper I went into AI, the more a personal question kept nagging me: is college still worth it for my kids? Students pick from 10-15 careers they've heard of when more than 900 exist, schools funnel everyone toward degrees, and nobody tells them which paths AI is about to reshape. So I built Future Me, Answered, a career discovery platform that starts with who you are, treats college, trades, military and direct-to-work as equals, and includes an AI Resilience signal grounded in Labor Department data and published AI-exposure research, so students see how exposed each path is before they invest.

Why I Built This
A parent's concern about the ROI of college in the AI era

41% of college graduates end up underemployed. Schools funnel nearly everyone toward a four-year degree when trades, military and direct-to-work paths offer great opportunities that get overlooked entirely. And nobody is telling students which of those paths are about to be disrupted by the same AI tools I'm using every day.

"What do you want to be when you grow up?" is one of the hardest questions anyone faces, and the tools to help answer it haven't kept up. I wanted to give kids facing that question a better answer, one that's honest about where AI is headed and treats every path with equal respect.

How It Works: 5-Stage Framework
Discover, Explore, Compare, Test, Decide

I designed a 5-stage instructional framework grounded in learning science principles I've applied across my career (and formalized through my M.S. in Instructional Technology):

  1. Discover: 15 behavioral questions surface who you are, your strengths, interests, values, environment preferences. AI generates a shareable identity archetype.
  2. Explore: AI suggests a personalized set of diverse career paths backed by O*NET data (more than 900 occupations) and Bureau of Labor Statistics salary information. Every suggestion includes an AI Resilience signal, a Strong, Moderate or Watch read built from U.S. Labor Department task data and published AI-exposure research. The more paths they dismiss, the smarter the suggestions get. No sugarcoating.
  3. Compare: A weighted decision matrix where students set their own priorities (salary, growth, lifestyle, AI resilience) and AI scores each path against their criteria.
  4. Test: Real-world experiments like shadow days, informational interviews and apprenticeships. AI reflects on what they learned and how it validates or contradicts their assumptions.
  5. Decide: A concrete 30-day action plan with next steps, timelines and accountability.
Design Decisions That Define It
Equal paths, honest AI assessment, real data, multi-stakeholder architecture

Equal paths. College, trades, military and direct-to-work are presented as equals. Every AI suggestion round must include multiple path types. No hierarchy, no "fallback" label. A licensed electrician and a software engineer get the same visual treatment, the same data depth and the same respect.

Honest, research-grounded AI assessment. Every career carries an AI Resilience signal built from U.S. Labor Department task data (the human judgment and physical presence each job demands) and published AI-exposure research, not an AI opinion. If a path is highly exposed to automation, students see that clearly before investing four years and six figures.

Real data. Every suggestion is backed by O*NET (U.S. Department of Labor, more than 900 occupations) and Bureau of Labor Statistics salary and growth data. Verified labor market information.

Multi-stakeholder. Students navigate the journey. Counselors manage more than 400 student cohorts and send "Spark" nudges to students who stall. Parents access scoped portals. Administrators configure AI models and prompts. FERPA-aligned architecture, built for school district adoption.

How It Was Built
AI orchestration with Claude Code, production-grade architecture

I conceived and designed the product, the 5-stage framework and the instructional approach. I orchestrated AI using Claude Code to build the platform. My brother, Mike Pointer, is co-founder and engineered the production backend, the databases and security that made it real.

Tech stack: Next.js, React, TypeScript, Vercel AI SDK with multi-provider LLM abstraction (Claude as the primary provider, with Gemini, GPT-4o and Amazon Bedrock switchable per task from an admin dashboard). AI prompts are stored in the database and editable without deploying code, with per-prompt model and temperature overrides. O*NET and CareerOneStop APIs for real labor market data, Stripe for payments, Zod-validated AI outputs and FERPA-aligned architecture with role-based access control across four user types.

This is the same pattern as CareerSpark: I use AI to build real products. The difference is that CareerSpark was built for an enterprise. Future Me, Answered was built from a parent's kitchen table because the problem mattered enough to solve on my own time.

CareerPointers

AI Career Coaching Across the Whole Job Search

11 Module Journey
4 AI Agent Panel
3 Languages Shipped

CareerPointers is an 11-module career development platform that carries a person from self-assessment through salary negotiation, and then keeps working after the journey ends: a portfolio that adapts per employer, a resume manager, a four-agent AI interview simulator and an applicant CRM that runs for as long as the search takes. Every tool feeds from one Story Bank, so the story a recruiter reads is the story told in the interview room. If you arrived at this page from a link with your company's name on it, and a resume written for that company loaded, you have seen the idea working. I built this site and the assistant on it myself, start to finish, and I am working now on having CareerPointers generate it.

Why I Built This
A story bank I built for myself and colleagues who needed it more than I did

Building the Story Bank for myself is what made the rest click. I kept rewriting the same accomplishment differently for every resume, every cover letter, every interview answer, until I built one place to capture each story once. That is when I understood what should have been obvious. Stories are what people actually remember about you, not job titles or skill lists, and they are what you have to practice telling, over and over, to different people, for different reasons. Every other tool in CareerPointers exists to feed from that one bank or to help someone rehearse what is in it.

I learned the underlying lesson at TIAA. I spent two years there on AI capability work, and the thing that stayed with me is that access is not adoption. Handing people a tool is the easy part. Career guidance only changes anything when it is personal, and personal does not scale through a portal. It scales when the coaching is built into the journey itself. That conviction is what I carried out of the building. The product is my own.

I left TIAA on July 1 after fifteen years, and I was not the only one who left. I had spent years helping colleagues there think about their careers, and I could no longer help them from a role I no longer had. I built CareerPointers to get that help to them in a form that did not depend on me being in the room.

How It Works: Build, Deliver, Practice
An 11-module journey, a portfolio that adapts per employer, an interview room

The journey is 11 modules, from Know Yourself through Negotiate Your Worth, and it is linear by design. Each module produces something the next one uses, so the values a person names in module one shape the summary their resume module writes five modules later. That is architecture, not coincidence.

Deliver turns what they have built into an employer-personalized portfolio. One content library, rendered with a different theme, block selection and resume version per company through a magic link, so a recruiter at a healthcare company and a recruiter at a fintech see two honest presentations of one source of truth.

Practice is the Hot Seat, a four-agent AI mock interview. A job analyst reads the posting, a question designer writes the questions that posting would actually produce, an interviewer agent reacts and follows up in real time, and a coach returns a scored report. It rehearses the room before the room happens.

Design Decisions That Define It
Show the reasoning, keep one source of truth, teach on every screen

Show the reasoning behind every score. Every AI-scored moment in the product, interview performance and career path fit alike, shows the factors behind the number and what would move it. A confidence score with no inspectable reasoning is just asking the user to trust me. This product is built to survive a user who argues back.

One story, everywhere. A user's STAR stories live in a single bank, encrypted at rest. The resume, the portfolio and the interview prep all draw from it, so the story a recruiter reads matches the story told in the interview room.

Teach something on every screen. Each module is built to develop the user's own thinking, their values, their positioning, their stories. A screen that only gathers data without teaching anything does not ship.

How It Was Built
AI-directed development, a production stack and a co-founder who engineered the backend

I conceived the product and designed the platform, the 11-module journey, the coaching approach and the product decisions behind it. I direct AI, primarily Claude Code, to write production code, and I have shipped a real share of this codebase that way. My brother, Mike Pointer, is co-founder and engineered the production backend, the databases and security that made it real. It is the same partnership that built Future Me, Answered. What I do is scope the problem, tell a good technical answer from a bad one and keep the build moving to the finish.

Tech stack: Next.js 15, React 19 and TypeScript, deployed on Vercel. Turso and Drizzle for the database, Better-auth for authentication, Stripe for checkout. An in-house AI gateway with a database-driven prompt library, rather than a bolted-on SDK, powers the four-agent interview simulator, the Story Bank with encrypted STAR fields, the applicant CRM and a resume manager with real PDF and DOCX export. It ships in English, Spanish and French.

The pattern is the point. I use AI to build real products instead of waiting for someone else to build them for me. This site is the version with no one else's hands on it. joepointer.com, the magic links, the assistant that answers questions about my background and the per-employer resume delivery are mine end to end, and they are the working demo of the platform.

Revolver LCMS

Designing Enterprise Content for Infinite Reuse

Systematic Content Reuse
3 Fortune 500 Clients
Global Deployment

In 2001, companies were rebuilding the same training content multiple times: for web, for paper, for mobile, for different audiences. I helped design Revolver LCMS, a learning platform that separated content from delivery. Authors entered knowledge once, the platform published everywhere. Three Fortune 500 clients deployed it globally, reducing redundant development effort. Two decades before "headless content" became an industry term.

The Market Problem & Innovation
See the 2001 market moment and the core insight: separate what you're teaching from how you're teaching it

The Market Moment (2001)

In the early 2000s, companies were moving training from classrooms to networks and the internet. But they faced a critical problem: they were building content in silos. A course built for web-based learners couldn't easily become a paper job aid. Training teams rebuilt the same knowledge multiple times, for different mediums, different contexts, different audiences.

The Insight: Content Separation

Separate what you're teaching from how you're teaching it. Before Revolver, content and delivery were locked together. Changing the look and feel meant rebuilding the entire course. Delivering to a new audience meant starting over. Revolver inverted that model. Authors entered content once. The platform published everywhere.

How It Worked

The same learning object could be deployed across multiple contexts:

  • For web-based learners: Full, interactive lesson with exercises and explanations
  • For field technicians: Condensed job aid delivered on paper
  • For mobile users: Micro-learning "nuggets" optimized for phones
  • For subject matter experts: Expert review in short, straightforward format
  • For novice learners: Expanded lessons with detailed explanations and practice

And the entire look and feel of a course? That was a design choice applied at publishing time, not during authoring.

My Role & Impact
See how I designed knowledge structure for global enterprises as a translator between learning science and technology

My Role: Knowledge Architect + Translator

I designed how enterprises should think about knowledge structure. My unique blend: Knowledge Architecture (structured content for reuse), Instructional Design (built learning science into the structure), UX Design (made structured thinking easy, not bureaucratic), and Global Trainer & Sales Engineer (traveled the world teaching enterprises how to think differently about knowledge as organizational infrastructure).

Separation of Concerns

This principle (separating content from context, delivery and design) was radical for 2001. Today, we call this "API-first thinking" or "headless content." Revolver was doing it two decades early.

For enterprises, this meant a reduction in redundant development effort, faster time-to-publish (days instead of weeks), consistency across contexts, sustainability (content stayed evergreen and reusable) and scalability (large organizations could manage knowledge as infrastructure).

Why This Work Still Matters
See how Revolver's principle shapes everything I do today: systems first, humans always

Revolver demonstrated a principle that shapes everything I do today. The best systems don't force people into one way of working, they let knowledge and design scale according to context.

That principle shows up in CareerSpark (content adapts to learner level and style), my workplace modernization work (one design for multiple contexts) and my core operating principle: "Systems First, Humans Always." I learned early that transformation happens when you separate what needs to happen from how it happens.

306% Growth in Associates Recognized, 2021

Client Services became the largest adopter and user of TIAA's recognition platform because of the culture we built. When TIAA decided to overhaul its enterprise-wide R&R system, that track record earned me a seat on the corporate platform selection team.

The Program Story
See how Client Services grew from a manual, mailbox-driven process to leading the entire enterprise in recognition activity

Starting Point

I took the program over in 2014, when the previous administrator was reallocated during a reorganization. Nominations arrived in a shared mailbox and awards were tracked by hand, so the Human Capital delegates in each business area were spending their time on data entry rather than on recognizing anyone. The friction was the problem, and it was suppressing use through exactly the change cycles TIAA was navigating.

Building the Culture

The work was not about administering a program. It was about changing behavior at scale. For more than 10 years of leading the program, I built and negotiated the annual engagement budget with Finance and grew it to $300,000, administered roughly $500,000 in total annual recognition spend across more than 3,000 associates once the enterprise points allocation is counted, created recognition frameworks that made it easier for managers to act consistently, and built rhythms and visibility that turned sporadic recognition into an organizational habit. The results reflected that shift. 2020 was already up 27% over 2019, and in 2021 we grew another 306% in associates recognized, 8,987 additional recognitions, in the first year on the new enterprise platform I helped select and launch. Client Services became the largest user of that platform, eventually accounting for 34% of all recognition activity across the company in 2021.

Why It Worked

Recognition programs fail when they stay administrative. This one succeeded because I treated it as an adoption problem. The platform was a tool. The real work was helping leaders understand why consistent recognition drives retention, how to make it genuine rather than performative and how to build it into their existing rhythms rather than bolting it on as an extra task.

The Platform Selection
See how the program work led to a seat on TIAA's corporate R&R platform selection team

The Invitation

When TIAA decided to replace its enterprise-wide recognition platform, the corporate team came to me. Client Services was the biggest user of the existing system, and my track record of driving adoption made me the right voice in the room. I was brought onto the corporate selection team to represent the practitioner perspective, someone who had actually used the platform at scale and understood what worked and what didn't.

The Selection Process

As a member of the team, I helped prepare the RFP, evaluated vendor proposals and participated in the final platform selection. The evaluation criteria balanced features against adoption likelihood. What good looked like on paper was not always what would work for a large, distributed workforce with varying levels of manager engagement.

Implementation and Launch

After selection, I joined the implementation team to ensure the transition went smoothly. The new platform launched company-wide, replacing the legacy system across all of TIAA. From there, I directly supported the corporate administrator of the platform and served as a key Recognition Champion across the company, helping drive adoption and consistency in how recognition was practiced at every level of the organization.

Why This Work Matters
See the connection between recognition culture and organizational change capacity

Recognition is an adoption problem. Every major change initiative, from AI adoption to workplace modernization to new platform rollouts, succeeds or fails based on whether people feel seen for the effort they're putting in. Organizations that recognize behavior change early get more of it. Organizations that don't wonder why adoption stalls.

The R&R program work and the AI adoption work are the same work: changing behavior at organizational scale requires building the conditions where people feel safe to try, valued when they do and motivated to keep going. Recognition is a direct tool for a leader managing that kind of change.

The platform selection chapter added a second dimension: the ability to represent practitioner needs in a procurement process, evaluate vendor claims against real-world use and shepherd an enterprise-wide technology transition from selection through launch.

How I measure adoption

STAGE ONE

Access

The AI rollout reporting I have seen stops at who was given access. Access is a count. It tells you the licenses were bought and the switch was flipped.

STAGE TWO

Usage

Usage is the floor. At TIAA I measured active use rather than headcount with access, and I reported the difference rather than the flattering number.

STAGE THREE

Transforming the way you work

Whether a manager can see someone doing the work differently in the 90 days after the tool shows up, and that takes longer to see than a usage report.

What I want to be measured on

Stage three is Kirkpatrick Level 3, which learning teams have run since the 1950s. What is new is running it on an AI rollout, where the reporting goes to the team that shipped the tool and I have not seen anyone assigned to do the watching.

Adoption-first design for the people who have to change how they work.

How I Think About Work

The principles I apply to every transformation challenge

The business should lead the "what." Technology should lead the "how." When that order gets reversed, you get solutions looking for problems.

How I Design Systems

Systems First, Humans Always

I think systematically about how organizations work, from workflows and incentives to infrastructure and governance. But I never optimize the system at the expense of the people in it. I think of it as human in the lead, not just human in the loop.

+ See How I Apply This

How I operationalize it: In Revolver (2001), I separated what you're teaching from how you're teaching it. The same content could publish to web, mobile, paper, expert review or novice learner, each optimized for context. The SYSTEM scaled content reuse. The HUMAN (the learner) always stayed at the center.

In CareerSpark, AI surfaces career options, but humans decide. The system recommends; people choose. Same principle, different era.

The proof: Implemented Revolver at three Fortune 500 companies, reducing content development time through systematic reuse. CareerSpark went company-wide, and the part I judge it on is that the people who used it were solving something they already cared about.

How I Think About AI

AI as Amplifier, Not Replacement

AI does not replace what you know. It makes what you know more valuable. The real skill is knowing what good looks like and being able to guide AI to get there.

+ See How I Apply This

How I operationalize it: CareerSpark teaches AI through the one subject every associate is already an expert in: themselves. Instead of "Here's how to use AI for business cases," I made it "Let's use AI to see your skills, explore your options and build your story." A 27-year associate terrified she had nothing to offer watched AI break down her work into more than 20 transferable skills. Her face lit up. AI did not replace her judgment. It helped her see what she already had but could not articulate.

The proof: Confidence increased. Fear decreased. AI became a tool for self-discovery, not a threat. That is what amplification looks like: helping people see what they are capable of.

The Human Side of Transformation

That Is Not Resistance. That Is Identity.

When someone says "I like doing that work myself," they are not resisting the technology. They are protecting the thing that makes them feel valuable. Until you address that, no amount of training will drive adoption.

+ See How I Apply This

A colleague said that to me. I suggested an AI tool that could do a piece of her work, and that was her answer.

How I operationalize it: With CareerSpark, I did not teach people how to use AI by starting with the technology. I started with something they already cared about: themselves. I showed them how AI could help with their own career development. That was the moment the light bulb went on. Once they understood what AI could do for them personally, they started seeing how it could apply to their work on their own.

The same approach will not work for everyone. Some people need to see a demo. Some need to try it on something personal first. Some just need time. The shift happens when you find the right moment and the right approach for that person.

The proof: Live sessions that reached more than 1,500 people, meeting them where they were. Real conversations about real uncertainty, not training sessions. That is the work most organizations skip, and it is the reason adoption either takes hold or quietly dies.

DAYS
1-30

Adoption Audit

  • Shadow teams to understand actual workflows vs. intended processes

DAYS
31-60

Pilot Design & Launch

Show adoption working on something your leadership is watching

  • Pick the proving ground together, a new initiative or one already in flight that is stuck

  • Recruit internal champions and early adopters across functions
  • Establish measurement that tracks behavior change, not logins
By day 60 you have a live pilot with early results you can measure and a written approach your teams can repeat.

61-90

  • Present findings and recommendations to leadership
By day 90 you have a playbook for keeping adoption going at scale and a leadership presentation that lays out the path forward.
